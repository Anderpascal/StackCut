## The Question Every CFO Is Asking

How much should we spend on software? It sounds simple. It is not. SaaS spend is highly variable by industry, company stage, and go-to-market motion. A developer-tool company and a brick-and-mortar retailer with identical headcounts might have SaaS budgets that differ by a factor of five.

But benchmarks exist. And knowing them is the difference between a CFO who can defend every line item and one who gets surprised at board reviews.

This guide gives you the numbers — by stage, by category, by headcount — and tells you what red flags to look for in your own stack.

## The Top-Level Benchmark: SaaS Spend as a Percent of Revenue

The most commonly cited benchmark is SaaS spend as a percentage of annual recurring revenue or total revenue.

| Company Stage | Typical SaaS Spend as % of Revenue |
|---|---|
| Pre-revenue / Seed | N/A (absolute spend matters more) |
| Early-stage ($1M–$5M ARR) | 15–25% of ARR |
| Growth stage ($5M–$50M ARR) | 8–15% of ARR |
| Scale ($50M–$200M ARR) | 5–10% of ARR |
| Enterprise ($200M+ ARR) | 3–7% of ARR |

As revenue grows, SaaS spend should grow slower — efficiency is the hallmark of a mature software procurement function. If your SaaS spend as a percent of revenue has stayed flat or grown as you scaled, that is a red flag.

## Per-Employee SaaS Spend Benchmarks

Headcount-normalized spend is the most useful operational metric because it accounts for company size without requiring a revenue figure.

| Industry | SaaS Spend Per Employee Per Year (2026) |
|---|---|
| Software / SaaS companies | $5,500 – $9,000 |
| Financial services | $4,200 – $7,500 |
| Professional services | $3,000 – $5,500 |
| Healthcare | $2,800 – $5,000 |
| Retail / e-commerce | $1,800 – $3,500 |
| Manufacturing | $1,200 – $2,800 |

Software companies spend more because their entire product workflow — development, testing, deployment, monitoring, collaboration — runs on SaaS. If you are a software company spending below $4,000 per employee, you are either unusually lean or you have significant shadow IT that is not showing up in your accounting.

## Category-Level Benchmarks

Breaking down by category reveals where overspend hides. These percentages represent share of total SaaS budget.

### Infrastructure and DevOps: 30–45%

For software companies, cloud infrastructure (AWS, GCP, Azure) plus DevOps tooling is the largest category. Non-tech companies typically sit at 15–20%. If infrastructure exceeds 50% of your SaaS budget at a non-technical company, you likely have a cloud optimization problem separate from your SaaS problem.

### Sales and Marketing: 20–30%

CRM, outbound tools, advertising platforms, SEO tools, marketing automation, and the long tail of growth experiments. This category is the most prone to sprawl because sales and marketing teams have budget discretionary authority and a high tolerance for trying new tools. Benchmark: if your sales and marketing SaaS spend per account executive exceeds $3,500 per year (excluding CRM), you probably have redundancy.

### Productivity and Collaboration: 8–15%

Email, calendar, document management, project management, communication tools. This category is often over-purchased because every team wants their own preferred tool. Anything above 15% suggests you have not consolidated around a platform (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, or Atlassian) and are paying for fragmented alternatives.

### HR and Finance: 5–10%

HRIS, payroll, benefits administration, expense management, accounting software. The platform consolidation trend (Rippling, Deel, Gusto) has compressed this category. If you are paying for more than three separate HR tools, you almost certainly have overlap.

### Security and Compliance: 10–18%

Growing. SOC 2, GDPR, and customer security questionnaires are forcing even small companies to buy security tooling earlier than they historically would have. This category tends to be sticky — security tools are hard to rip out — so getting the right tools at the right tier from the start matters more here than anywhere else.

## Red Flags That Signal Overspend

Numbers on their own are inert. Here are the patterns that consistently indicate a SaaS budget problem:

### 1. More Tools Than Employees in Any Single Category

If you have 50 employees and 60 SaaS tools in your productivity and collaboration category, you have a proliferation problem. Tool count per employee should never exceed 1:1 in any category.

### 2. Seat Counts Lagging Headcount by More Than 20%

If your paid seat count across the stack exceeds your current headcount by more than 20%, you are carrying seats from departed employees. At $340 per ghost seat per year (industry average), this adds up fast. A 100-person company that grew from 80 and never cleaned up its seats is paying for roughly 6–10 ghost seats per tool — across potentially 40+ tools.

### 3. Auto-Renewal Surprises Above $5,000

Any renewal that catches your finance team off-guard above $5,000 is a governance failure. It means your renewal calendar is not working. One surprise auto-renewal per quarter at the median mid-market company costs $18,000 to $35,000 per year in unplanned, unoptimized spend.

### 4. No Downgrade in the Last 24 Months

If your stack has not seen a single plan downgrade in two years, you are not reviewing utilization. Tool usage changes as teams change. Features you needed at 30 people often go unused at 100. Run the [Downgrade Engine](/downgrade) to identify which tools you are oversubscribed on.

### 5. SaaS Budget Set Annually With No Mid-Year Review

SaaS spend changes faster than an annual budget cycle. Teams add tools, people leave, products pivot. A budget set in January with no review until December is a budget that is wrong by August. Quarterly reviews are the minimum viable cadence for spend management.

## How to Use These Benchmarks Operationally

Benchmarks are not targets — they are guardrails. If you are below the benchmark in every category, do not immediately spend up to it. If you are above it in one category, start there.

The right process:

1. Pull your current per-category spend from your accounting system
2. Compare to the benchmarks above (adjusted for your industry and stage)
3. For every category 10%+ above benchmark, build a utilization report
4. Prioritize categories by total dollar overage, not percentage overage
5. Set a 90-day reduction target for the top two categories

## The Bottom Line

Every company has a right number for software spend. The companies that know their number — and can defend it line by line — make better procurement decisions, negotiate better contracts, and waste less money on tools that gather dust. Pick a benchmark, measure against it quarterly, and treat any overage as a red-pen item that needs a justification or a cut.
