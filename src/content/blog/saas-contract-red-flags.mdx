## The Fine Print Is Where Vendors Make Their Margin

SaaS contracts are not drafted to protect you. They are drafted by vendor legal teams optimized for maximum revenue capture, minimum flexibility for the buyer, and maximum friction at cancellation. Most buyers sign them after a 10-minute skim, focused on the price line and the features list.

That is exactly what vendors count on.

The following 12 red flags are the clauses, structures, and omissions that appear most often in SaaS agreements — and cost companies real money when they go unnoticed. Mark them in red before you countersign.

## Red Flag 1: Auto-Renewal With a Short Cancellation Window

The most common and most expensive clause in SaaS contracts. The agreement auto-renews for another full term unless you provide written notice to cancel within a window — often 30, 60, or 90 days before the renewal date. Miss the window by a day and you owe another year.

What to push for: a minimum 90-day cancellation window, or a mutual opt-in renewal requirement for contracts over $10,000 annually. At minimum, calendar the cancellation deadline the moment you sign.

## Red Flag 2: Unilateral Price Increase Rights

Some contracts include language like "Vendor reserves the right to adjust pricing with 30 days' notice." This means your $50,000/year contract can become $65,000 with a short email and no renegotiation right.

What to push for: cap annual price increases explicitly in the contract — typically tied to CPI (Consumer Price Index) plus 2–3 percentage points. Any increase beyond that should require written consent from your authorized signatory.

## Red Flag 3: Vague Definitions of "Active User" or "Seat"

Per-seat contracts that do not precisely define what constitutes a billable user create a liability. If the vendor's system counts a provisioned account (even if never logged in) as a seat, or counts API service accounts, you will be overbilled.

Push for explicit language: a seat is an individual human user who has authenticated at least once in the billing period, excluding system accounts, service accounts, and integrations.

## Red Flag 4: Overage Charges Without Hard Caps

Usage-based elements — API calls, data storage, email sends, workflow executions — that include overage pricing but no hard spend cap are a budget risk. A misconfigured integration or a viral campaign can generate charges that exceed your base contract within a week.

Negotiate a hard cap equal to 150% of your expected usage and require vendor notification before charges exceed 120% of the base plan. Never sign a contract with open-ended overage exposure.

## Red Flag 5: Data Portability Restrictions at Offboarding

Some contracts — particularly in CRM, marketing automation, and data platforms — restrict how and when you can export your data at cancellation. Watch for clauses that limit export to CSV only, restrict historical data export, or impose time limits (e.g., "data available for 30 days post-termination").

Your data is yours. The contract should state explicitly that you can export all data in machine-readable format (not just CSV) within 90 days of termination, with no additional charge.

## Red Flag 6: Minimum Commitment Floors That Expand

Some enterprise contracts include a minimum seat or usage floor that automatically ratchets up each year ("no less than the prior year's licensed seats"). This means even if your team shrinks, you cannot reduce your commitment below your peak usage.

Push for an explicit right to reduce seat counts at each annual renewal to your actual active user count from the prior 90 days. If the vendor refuses, treat the floor as a hard cost and include it in your [ROI calculation](/roi) before signing.

## Red Flag 7: Automatic Upsell on Feature Unlocks

Watch for contracts where specific features are "included" at your tier but trigger an automatic upgrade if used above a threshold. Marketing automation platforms are notorious for this: a feature is bundled, but sending more than 10,000 emails or triggering more than 5 automated workflows in a month auto-upgrades you to the next tier.

The fix: define all feature limits numerically in the order form itself, not by reference to a "current plan details" page that the vendor can update unilaterally.

## Red Flag 8: Liability Cap Below Your Contract Value

Standard vendor contracts cap their total liability to you at one month of fees paid. If the vendor suffers a data breach, an extended outage, or causes material harm to your business, their maximum exposure is $5,000 on a $60,000/year contract.

For any tool touching customer data or core operations, negotiate the liability cap up to at least 12 months of fees paid, and ensure it does not cap liability for data breaches covered by applicable privacy law.

## Red Flag 9: Indemnification That Flows Only One Way

One-sided indemnification clauses require you to defend the vendor against third-party claims arising from your use of their software, while providing you no reciprocal protection. Specifically watch for clauses where you indemnify the vendor against claims related to your data — this can expose you in breach or IP disputes.

Standard: indemnification should be mutual. The vendor covers their product; you cover your data and your use of the product.

## Red Flag 10: SLA Credits That Do Not Offset Real Costs

A 99.9% uptime SLA sounds strong until you read the remedies section. Many contracts offer service credits — 10% of one month's fee — for SLA breaches. On a $5,000/month contract, a 4-hour outage during peak business hours earns you a $500 credit against next month's invoice.

Negotiate for SLA credits that scale with the severity and duration of the outage, and for the right to terminate the contract without penalty if the vendor misses SLA thresholds in two consecutive months.

## Red Flag 11: Governing Law and Dispute Resolution in Vendor-Friendly Jurisdictions

Mandatory arbitration clauses and governing law provisions that require disputes to be handled in the vendor's home state or country are designed to make litigation impractical for buyers. Binding arbitration clauses often waive your right to class action participation.

Push for governing law in your jurisdiction, or a neutral jurisdiction if the vendor refuses. At minimum, ensure arbitration is not the exclusive remedy for claims under a threshold dollar amount.

## Red Flag 12: "Acceptable Use" Clauses Tied to Competitive Benchmarking

Some SaaS contracts — particularly in analytics, data, and infrastructure — include acceptable use policies that prohibit using the software to benchmark it against competitors, publish performance comparisons, or share benchmark data with third parties. This is a clause designed to prevent you from using comparison tools and cost-benchmarking services like [StackCut's pricing comparisons](/compare).

Flag any clause that restricts your right to compare, benchmark, or share performance data with your own advisors and procurement tools. You have a legitimate business interest in knowing whether you are paying market rates.

## How to Use This List in Practice

Do not wait for a legal review to surface these issues. Before the contract goes to counsel, do a first pass yourself:

1. Use Ctrl+F to search for: "auto-renew," "price increase," "overage," "minimum commitment," "liability," "indemnif," "arbitration," "acceptable use," and "termination."
2. Flag every match and note whether the clause is bilateral or one-sided.
3. Build a redline list ranked by financial exposure — start negotiation from the highest-risk items.

For contracts over $25,000 annually, every hour spent on this review returns many multiples in avoided costs. You can also use the [StackCut negotiation guide](/negotiation) to build specific counter-language for each of these clauses.

If you are approaching a renewal rather than a new signature, the same checklist applies — renewals typically carry forward the original terms unless you explicitly renegotiate them. Do not assume your position improved.

## Bottom Line

SaaS contracts are not take-it-or-leave-it. Vendors expect redlines. The buyers who skip the review are subsidizing the ones who do not. Read the fine print, flag the 12 clauses above, and negotiate before ink dries — because after signature, every one of these red flags becomes a feature of your contract, not a bug.
